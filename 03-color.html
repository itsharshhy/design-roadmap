Colour is a feeling you can measure.

Colour is usually the first thing people notice and the first thing beginners get wrong. This chapter covers the science, the psychology (and the myths about it), how brands pick colours, and how to build a palette that works.

3.1 · The basics

Every colour has three dials

Forget the 16 million colours. Designers think in three dials. Once you know them you can describe and fix any colour.

Hue

Which colour it is: red, blue, green. The position on the wheel.

Saturation

How intense it is, from grey (0%) to vivid (100%). Low saturation feels calm and premium; high feels loud and young.

Lightness (value)

How dark or light. The most important dial: contrast and readability live here.

Explain like I'm 10

Hue is which crayon you pick. Saturation is how hard you press. Lightness is whether you add black or white paint to it.

Screen vs paper

RGB

Red, Green, Blue light. Used for screens. Mixing all of them gives white.

CMYK

Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, Black ink. Used for print. Bright screen colours often print duller.

HEX / Pantone

#E2462B is just RGB written as code. Pantone is a numbered ink system so a brand's red is the same everywhere.

Research · Exciting red, competent blue

In four studies, Labrecque & Milne found hue, saturation and lightness each shift perceived brand personality. Red raised perceived excitement, blue raised competence, and colour changed purchase intent. They also note that blue is the most common logo colour.

Labrecque, L. I. & Milne, G. R. (2012). Exciting red and competent blue. Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, 40(5), 711–727.
Research · Fit beats favourite

Bottomley & Doyle found people judge logo colours by how appropriate they are for the product. Functional products (tools, banks) suit functional colours like blue and grey. Sensory and social products (food, fashion) suit warm, expressive ones. There's no single "best" colour, only the best fit.

Bottomley, P. A. & Doyle, J. R. (2006). The interactive effects of colors and products on perceptions of brand logo appropriateness. Marketing Theory, 6(1).
Myth check · "90% of judgements are colour"

You'll see "62–90% of snap judgements are based on colour alone" everywhere. It comes from Singh (2006), a literature review, not a new experiment. Treat it as "colour matters a lot in first impressions", not a precise number. Psychologists Elliot & Maier also stress that colour effects depend heavily on context: red means "danger" on a sign and "love" on a card.

Singh, S. (2006). Impact of color on marketing. Management Decision, 44(6). · Elliot, A. J. & Maier, M. A. (2014). Color psychology. Annual Review of Psychology, 65.

3.5 · How brands decide

The colour decision process

Professionals don't pick a colour because it's their favourite. They follow a process, roughly this one:

  1. Write 3–5 personality words

    "Playful, honest, fast" leads to very different colours than "calm, expert, premium".

  2. Map the competitors

    Lay out the logos of 10 competitors. If they're all blue (like banks), you have a choice: fit in to signal trust, or break out to be remembered. Pepsi went blue to stand apart from Coke's red. T-Mobile owns magenta in a sea of red and blue.

  3. Check the culture

    Where is the audience? White, red and green mean different things in different markets.

  4. Pick one hero colour

    A single ownable colour is worth more than five. Think Tiffany blue, Cadbury purple, UPS brown. (Some are even trademarked.)

  5. Build the system around it

    Neutrals for text and backgrounds, one accent, plus "semantic" colours: green for success, red for errors, yellow for warnings.

  6. Test it

    Check contrast, colour-blind views and print, and see it on a phone in sunlight. Then show real people.

3.6 · The 60-30-10 rule

Don't use your colours equally

Borrowed from interior design: about 60% dominant (usually a neutral), 30% secondary and 10% accent. The accent is your "red jumper", reserved for the things you want clicked or noticed.

Hover the bar: when the accent gets bigger, it stops being special.

3.7 · Contrast & access

If they can't read it, it isn't design

Around 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have some form of colour-vision deficiency. Plenty more people are reading on a cracked phone in sunlight. That's why the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) set minimum contrast ratios.

Contrast checker
Can you read me?

Body text needs to be comfortable for long reading, for everybody.

DoNever rely on colour alone. Add icons, labels or underlines (an error = red + ⚠ + text).
Don'tUse light grey text on white because it "looks clean". It's the most common contrast failure on the web.

Pro tip: avoid pure #000 on pure #FFF for long reading. A warm off-black on an off-white (like this page) is easier on the eyes and feels more crafted.

Your missions

Homework

  1. Steal from life

    Take 5 photos of things you love (a market, a sunset, a café). Use Coolors or Adobe Color "extract from image" to pull a palette from each.

  2. Competitor colour map

    Pick an industry (food delivery, banks, sneakers). Put 10 logos on one page. What's the dominant colour? Where's the gap?

  3. One poster, three moods

    Design one simple poster, then recolour it three ways: exciting, trustworthy, luxurious. Ask friends to match the mood words.

  4. Grey first

    Design your next layout in greys only. Add colour last. If it works in grey, it works.

Chapter five

Layout is deciding what matters most.

You now have colours and fonts. Layout is where you put them: what's big, what's small, what goes first, and how much space each thing gets to breathe.

5.1 · The four big principles
C.R.A.P.

Designer Robin Williams packed basic layout into four principles in The Non-Designer's Design Book. The acronym is silly on purpose so you never forget it.

Contrast

If two things aren't the same, make them very different. Big vs small, bold vs light. Timid differences look like mistakes.

Repetition

Reuse the same colours, fonts, shapes and spacing. Repetition creates a system, and a system feels professional.

Alignment

Nothing goes on the page randomly. Every element lines up with something else. Invisible lines create order.

Proximity

Group related things together and separate unrelated ones. (That's Gestalt from Chapter 02, put to work.)

Explain like I'm 10

Think of a school line-up. Teachers stand out (contrast), everyone wears the same uniform (repetition), kids stand in straight lines (alignment), and each class stands together (proximity). That's a layout!

5.2 · Hierarchy

Same words. Totally different poster.

Visual hierarchy is the order the eye reads things in. Without it, everything shouts at the same volume, which means nothing gets heard. Toggle it:

Hierarchy tools, from strongest to weakest: size → colour/contrast → weight → position → space around it → style.

Pro technique · The squint test

Squint (or blur the design) until you can't read the words. What do you still see first? If it's the right thing, your hierarchy works. Designers do this constantly.

5.3 · Grids

The invisible skeleton of every page

Swiss designer Josef Müller-Brockmann wrote the bible on this, Grid Systems in Graphic Design (1981). A grid is a set of invisible columns and rows. It makes alignment automatic and lets many pages feel like one family.

Single column

Books, blog posts, simple mobile screens. One block of text. Calm and focused.

Column grid (6 or 12)

Websites and magazines. 12 columns can be split into halves, thirds, quarters and sixths, which makes it super flexible.

Modular grid

Columns and rows make a field of boxes. Posters, dashboards and photo-heavy layouts. Elements span several modules.

Industry standard · The 8-point grid

Most product teams size and space everything in multiples of 8 (8, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64...). It divides cleanly on most screens and kills the endless "13px or 14px?" debate. Google's Material Design uses an 8dp grid with 4dp for small details.

5.4 · Composition

Where things sit changes how they feel

Same four shapes, three arrangements. Watch them move.

  • Centred / symmetrical: stable, formal, calm, but can feel dull.
  • Rule of thirds: put focal points where the third-lines cross. Natural and balanced, borrowed from photography.
  • Asymmetric: tension, energy, movement. Elements bleeding off the edge feel modern and confident.

About the golden ratio (1.618): it's a nice proportion, but claims that famous logos are "built on it" are mostly drawn on after the fact. Use it as a tool, not a religion.

5.5 · Whitespace

Empty space isn't wasted space

Whitespace (or "negative space") is any empty area, whatever its colour. Beginners fear it and fill every gap. Professionals use it on purpose.

Your missions

Homework

  1. Find the grid

    Screenshot 3 websites you like. In Figma, draw lines over every edge things align to. You'll uncover the grid.

  2. Three-poster drill

    Take one event (title, date, place, image) and make three posters: centred, rule-of-thirds and asymmetric.

  3. Redesign a bad flyer

    Find a cluttered local flyer or menu. Redesign it using C.R.A.P. Put the before and after side by side, because that's portfolio gold.

  4. Squint everything

    For one week, squint at every design you make before calling it done.

4.1 · Words first

Typeface, font, what?

A typeface is the design (Helvetica). A font is one specific file of it (Helvetica Bold Italic). A family is all the weights and styles together. Most people say "font" for everything, and that's fine.

Explain like I'm 10

A typeface is like a singer. Fonts are that singer whispering, shouting or singing in a deep voice. Same person, different moods.

The anatomy of a letter

The x-height matters most for beginners. Fonts with a tall x-height look bigger and read better at small sizes. That's why UI fonts usually have one.

4.2 · The families

Five personalities to know

There are hundreds of thousands of fonts, but almost all of them fall into a handful of groups. Each group carries a mood.

Serif

Little feet on the strokes. Descends from Roman stone carving and old printing. Feels traditional, trustworthy, literary, premium. Used by the New York Times, Vogue and law firms.

Timeless story

Sans-serif

No feet. Became popular in the 20th century. Feels modern, clean, neutral, friendly. The default for apps and tech: Google, Airbnb, Spotify.

Modern product

Slab serif

Chunky, block-like serifs. Feels sturdy, confident, a bit retro. Common on posters and in Western and industrial vibes.

Built Tough

Script

Looks handwritten. Feels personal, elegant or playful. Wedding invites, bakeries, signatures. Never use it for body text.

With love, Maya

Monospace

Every letter is the same width, like a typewriter. Feels technical, honest, nerdy. Code, tech brands, editorial labels like the ones on this site.

sys.boot()

Display

Made to be big: headlines and logos. Can be wild. Feels like whatever it was designed to feel. Use it once per design, at large sizes only.

4.3 · Type psychology

Fonts change what people believe

Research · Font appropriateness

Doyle & Bottomley found people were more likely to choose a brand when the font "fit" the product (a sturdy font for a tough product, a delicate one for a soft one) than when it didn't, even with identical names.

Doyle, J. R. & Bottomley, P. A. (2004). Font appropriateness and brand choice. Journal of Business Research, 57(8).
Research · Typefaces have personas

Brumberger showed readers consistently assign personalities to typefaces ("elegant", "friendly", "direct"). When the font's persona clashes with the text's tone, the message feels off.

Brumberger, E. R. (2003). The rhetoric of typography: The persona of typeface and text. Technical Communication, 50(2).
Research · Hard to read = hard to do

When exercise instructions were printed in a hard-to-read font, people estimated the routine would take 15.1 minutes instead of 8.2, and were less willing to try it. (A small study of 20 students, but it fits a large body of "processing fluency" research.) Readability changes how difficult things feel.

Song, H. & Schwarz, N. (2008). If it's hard to read, it's hard to do. Psychological Science, 19(10).
Explain like I'm 10

If your doctor wrote your prescription in bubbly party letters, you'd worry. If a birthday invite looked like a tax form, nobody would come. The letters have to match the moment.

4.4 · Readability lab

Line length and spacing, felt

Robert Bringhurst's classic The Elements of Typographic Style recommends roughly 45–75 characters per line for body text, with about 66 as ideal. Move the sliders and feel the difference.

Measure & leading

Typography exists to honour content. When the line is too long, the eye loses its place on the way back to the start of the next line. When it's too short, the reader is constantly interrupted and the rhythm turns choppy. Good leading, the space between lines, gives each line room to breathe without letting the paragraph fall apart. Change the sliders and notice how quickly reading goes from effortless to tiring. Nobody will ever compliment you on good line length, but everybody feels bad line length.

Leading

Space between lines. Body text: 1.4–1.6× the font size. Big headlines: tighter, 1.0–1.15×.

Tracking

Even spacing across a whole word or line. Add a little to ALL CAPS and tiny labels. Remove a little from big headlines.

Kerning

Space between one specific pair of letters, like "AV" or "To". Matters in logos and huge headlines.

4.5 · Type scale

Sizes that belong together

Don't pick sizes randomly (14, 17, 23, 31...). Pick a base size and multiply by a fixed ratio. Musical ratios like the "major third" (1.25) or "perfect fourth" (1.333) make sizes feel related, like notes in a chord.

Type scale generator

Rule of thumb: apps and dense interfaces suit small ratios (1.125–1.25). Posters and editorial landing pages suit big ones (1.333–1.618).

4.6 · Pairing

How to pair fonts without crying

  1. Start with one family

    One good family with several weights can do everything. Pairing is optional, not required.

  2. Two maximum

    One for headlines (personality), one for body (readability). A third is almost always a mistake.

  3. Contrast, don't conflict

    Pair things that are clearly different (serif + sans), never almost the same (two similar sans-serifs look like an accident).

  4. Match the bones

    Look for similar x-height and proportions so they sit together nicely on a line.

  5. Steal from the pros

    Fonts In Use shows real pairings by real designers. Start there instead of guessing.
